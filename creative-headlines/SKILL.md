# Creative headlines

Creativity belongs in only two places in conversion copy: the big idea and the headlines and crossheads (subheads). Everything else follows frameworks. This skill covers the headlines.

## Why headlines need to be creative

The first customer for any copy is the person who approves it, usually the CMO. Copy that never gets approved never converts. For the approver, copy has a functional job (convert) and a social job (make them look good, and impress the CEO). The headline is where you win them over.

Most four-to-six-word web headlines chase simplicity and clarity but give up **feeling**. The goal isn't an emotional headline. It's making the approver feel what they want to feel about their brand: proud, and seeing a brand others will envy. You want them to say "oh, cute" or "that's clever". That's the moment copy gets approved.

## Order of operations

1. Write the page with conversion copywriting first, using placeholder headlines and crossheads.
2. Then rewrite the headlines and crossheads with the methods below.

## Treat headlines as taglines

Familiar taglines show the pattern: "Make yourself. At home." "Shave time. Shave money." "What's strong with me." Each one borrows a phrase people already know and makes it work on more than one level.

Generate many options, dozens rather than three versions of one line, then pick the best.

### Method A: Idioms

1. Choose the thing you're writing for: a brand, product, service, or feature.
2. Write down three single words that express it:
   - one for its **category**
   - one for its **experience** (UX)
   - one for its **differentiator**
3. For each word, find about six idioms, sayings, or quotes that contain it. Let an idiom dictionary do the brainstorming instead of staring at the wall.
4. Try each one as a headline in context. Ask: does this line work on multiple levels? Keep the ones that get the "oh, cute" reaction.

**Antonym twist:** take each of your three words, find its opposite, find idioms for the opposite, and substitute your word in. "The whole wide world" becomes "The segmented wide world" for a segmentation product.

### Method B: Belief swap (when no idiom fits)

Take over a phrase your audience already believes to be true.

1. Ask: what does our market believe to be true, and how do they phrase it? What do we want them to believe instead?
2. Name the category by completing "Our product is a ___". Write down at least two categories.
3. For each category, gather at least six **positive** axioms, mottos, sayings, adages, or quotes that are accepted as true. Search "sayings about [category]"; image search and Pinterest help. The more common the saying, the better.
4. Substitute your brand name, feature name, or your own spin for the category word.

Examples of the pattern:
- "Open a can of soda" became "Open happiness".
- "Love is forever" became "A diamond is forever".
- "America runs on coffee" became "America runs on [brand]".
- "Engineers build products" became "Engineers build business".

Many substitutions won't work. That's fine; skip them. Avoid negative sayings, or ones that pick a fight with the category: they make the brand look bad when you substitute it in. A saying that doesn't contain the exact word can still spark an angle, so keep it in play.

### Method C: One-minute formula

When you have no time:

> Like a [category]. Unlike any [category].

Fill both blanks with the same generic name for the product or feature: "Like a cup of coffee. Unlike any cup of coffee." "Like a laptop. Unlike any laptop." It works almost universally, including as a crosshead for a common feature (e.g. a phone's camera section). The copy underneath must then explain what makes it unlike any other. Don't use it everywhere.

## Checks

- Try finalists in context on the page, not just in a list. Mark the ones that feel right as ready for validation, i.e. worth showing the approver.
- Clever is often confusing to most people, so always put a **clarifying payoff line** right next to a clever headline.
- Don't use this approach for paid social ad headlines; those work differently.
- Once the copy is live, run headline tests. Getting it live comes first.
- The same methods work beyond headlines, such as naming an event or campaign.

## Output

Return options grouped by method, each with its payoff line and a note on what levels it works on. Recommend one, and say why the approver will love it.
