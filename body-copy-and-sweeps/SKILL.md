# Body copy and sweeps

## Never start from a blank page

Pick a persuasion framework first. Open the doc, type the framework's section labels with space under each, and write there. Choose the framework from the reader's awareness stage and motivation (see `customer-research-for-copy`).

### Frameworks

- **PAS: Problem, Agitation, Solution.** Open with the problem in the reader's terms, agitate how it makes them feel, then solve it. Best when the reader is feeling a strong problem. Don't use it for a most-aware, high-intent reader, who doesn't need to hear the problem again.
- **DOS: Desire, Obstacle, Solution.**
  - **Desire:** what does the customer (not the marketer) want when they land? Make it real and vivid, intensifying the desire in a positive way, the way PAS intensifies a problem.
  - **Obstacle:** what's in their way? Often a belief about what it takes, such as thinking they need to overhaul everything, copy competitors, or give up.
  - **Solution:** it doesn't have to be the product right away. It can be the category or approach first.
  Works especially well in emails.
- **AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action.**
- **4Ps**, and modified versions of it.

## Draft the body

- Lead with "you", not "we", the brand name, or features.
- Lead with outcomes and benefits.
- Use the customer's own words from research.
- Match the opening to the reader's stage and motivation; a mismatch adds friction immediately.
- Use plain placeholder headlines; rewrite them later with `creative-headlines`.
- Length follows awareness and motivation, not a word count target. Don't cut persuasive copy just because someone thinks it's too long.

## Take-away sweeps

It's not just what you add to the page; it's also what you take away. Run these on your draft or on existing copy.

### Depletion-of-assets sweep

Find words that make the customer feel they're about to lose something: price, pricing, fee, cost, pay, charge, spend. You don't have to replace them all (e.g. with "invest"). Wherever one appears, immediately offset it with the value the customer gets, so the value feels bigger than the loss.

Check the design too. On many pricing pages the biggest elements are the word "Pricing" and the dollar amount, because of templates and design conventions. That draws the eye to losing money with no value in sight. Recommend shrinking the price, and giving the headline a job beyond the word "Pricing". Turn the sweep into an audit you can offer clients or the team.

### Phantom objections

Don't raise objections the reader doesn't have. Confirm an objection is real before putting it on the page. On web pages (sales, pricing, about) every reader sees it. Emails are more forgiving, because they can be triggered by what you know and ignored if irrelevant.

### Button anxiety

Do people feel safe clicking? Are there words on the button you don't need? Can the button reduce the friction of commitment ("Give [product] a try")? See `button-copy`.

### Social proof

Is the social proof actually proving anything? If it's just sitting on the page, it isn't doing the heavy lifting it could. Place it next to the claim it supports or cut it.

## Output

For drafts: the framework chosen and why, then the copy with section labels. For audits: a list of findings per sweep, each with the original line, the problem, and a suggested rewrite.
