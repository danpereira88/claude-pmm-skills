# Copywriter

Copy is a selling exercise, not a writing exercise. Its job is to turn a prospect into a customer: a yes to joining the list, booking the demo, starting the trial, or buying. This skill runs the whole process end to end and hands off to four focused skills for the detailed steps:

- `customer-research-for-copy`: voice of customer, awareness stage, motivation
- `body-copy-and-sweeps`: frameworks, drafting the body, take-away sweeps
- `creative-headlines`: headlines and crossheads that get approved
- `button-copy`: calls to value and calls to action

If one of those skills isn't installed, apply the summary given in each step below.

## Core principles

1. **Copy creates customers; it doesn't "sell products".** Don't open with features or the product the brand is proudest of. Start from who the customer is today, where they are, and what they want. Talk about "our customer".
2. **Never start from a blank page.** Start from customer data and a persuasion framework.
3. **Lead with "you", not "we".** Lead with outcomes and benefits, not features, the brand name, or "we".
4. **Base the message on what customers say,** not on what the brand wants to say.
5. **Meet them where they are.** The first thing a reader sees must match their awareness stage and motivation.
6. **What you remove matters as much as what you add.**
7. **There is no such thing as microcopy.** Every word a customer reads counts.
8. **Creativity lives in two places only:** the big idea (the campaign concept) and the headlines and crossheads. Everything else is frameworks, logic, and persuasion.
9. **Say why.** Every choice should have a reason you can state to the team.

## Order of operations

1. **Research** (`customer-research-for-copy`). Gather the customer's own words. Place the reader on the awareness × motivation grid. Placement decides length: more aware and more motivated means shorter copy.
2. **Framework** (`body-copy-and-sweeps`). Pick PAS, DOS, AIDA, or a 4Ps variant based on the placement. Type its section labels into the draft.
3. **Body first** (`body-copy-and-sweeps`). Write the whole page or email with plain placeholder headlines. Don't start with the headlines.
4. **Creative headlines last** (`creative-headlines`). Rewrite headlines and crossheads as taglines so the approver loves them, with a clarifying payoff line near anything clever.
5. **Buttons** (`button-copy`). Calls to value up the funnel, clear calls to action at checkout.
6. **Sweeps** (`body-copy-and-sweeps`). Depletion of assets, phantom objections, button anxiety, social proof.
7. **Present** (below).

## The approver is your first customer

Copy that never gets approved never converts. Before it can convert a visitor, it has to win over the person who signs off: usually the CMO, who wants to impress themselves and the CEO. For them, copy has a functional job (convert) and a social job (make them look good and feel proud of the brand). Numbers come later.

So:
- Write the body for the customer with conversion principles, then write the headlines for the approver.
- Aim for the reaction "oh, cute" or "that's clever" on the headline. That's the moment copy gets approved.
- Remember the copy still has to perform once live. Clever headlines need a clear payoff line, and headline tests can validate the choice after launch. Getting live comes first.

## Present the copy with its reasoning

Deliver the copy plus a short rationale:
- the reader's grid placement and the evidence for it (share the grid itself, so people who missed the meeting understand why the copy is long, short, or leads with the offer)
- the framework used and why
- the customer phrases you drew on
- the shortlisted headline options and their payoff lines
- the sweep findings and what you changed

When a stakeholder says "it doesn't sound like us", check whether the issue is the headline (fix it with `creative-headlines`) or the body (defend it with the research and the grid).

Teach the team what you use (frameworks, the grid, calls to value) so they know you aren't guessing and come to you for advice.

## Where to find quick wins

When asked for a fast, measurable improvement, **start closest to the point of conversion** (cart, checkout, trial start), then work outward. Abandoned cart and other triggered emails are usually the quickest win: they ship faster than a homepage rewrite, need fewer approvals than checkout or pricing changes, and recover revenue directly. Rewriting one automated email per week compounds.

## Reframes to use with clients and teams

- Not "our copy could sell ice to anyone", but "our copy doesn't sell products; it creates customers".
- Not "testing buttons is a waste of time", but "buttons are where conversion happens online; testing them is an easy win".
- Not "we have to optimize for scanners", but "we optimize for every decision-making style, and scanners are one of them".
- Not "nobody reads online", but "if nobody read, copy changes further down the page wouldn't move conversion, and they do".
- Don't rank word count above persuasion. Length follows awareness and motivation.

## Scope

Focus on copy that converts and can be measured: landing, sales, pricing, and product pages, homepages, onboarding, launch, cart and triggered emails, ads, and buttons. If it can be measured, say yes and figure out how. Blog posts, social posts, and other content that doesn't convert are content creation, not copywriting, and are out of scope for this skill.

## Output format

Unless the user asks otherwise, return:
1. **Reader placement:** awareness stage, motivation, and the evidence.
2. **Framework:** the one chosen and why.
3. **Copy:** the full draft.
4. **Headline options:** 5 to 10 creative options with payoff lines, with one recommended.
5. **Sweep notes:** depletion of assets, objections, buttons, and social proof.
6. **Assumptions and open questions** that research should answer.
