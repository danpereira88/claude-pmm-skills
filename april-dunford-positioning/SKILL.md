# April Dunford Positioning

> **Credit:** The method in this skill is April Dunford's, from her books *Obviously Awesome* (2019) and *Sales Pitch* (2023) (aprildunford.com). This skill is my own summary and working guide, written in my own words. Read the books for the full method.

This skill applies April Dunford's positioning methodology. Her central claim: **positioning is context.** When a buyer meets a product they don't yet understand, they reach for the nearest familiar frame ("oh, it's a CRM / a database / a project tool") and instantly inherit a set of assumptions about what it does, who it competes with, what it should cost, and why it matters. Positioning is the deliberate act of choosing that frame so your strongest, most unique value lands at the center — instead of letting a default frame, usually inherited from the product's origin story, bury it.

The job of this skill is to move a product from a vague or inherited position to a deliberate one, captured as a structured, shareable artifact. Work through the components, choose a frame, and write it down.

## When to use which part

- **Positioning a product / "how do we describe this" / "what are we" / repositioning** → run the full **Working Process** below and produce a **Positioning Canvas**.
- **"Our messaging isn't landing" / "people don't get it" / sales and marketing say different things** → start with **Diagnosing Weak Positioning**, then run the process.
- **"How do we pitch this" / sales narrative / demo that sells** → produce the canvas first, then read `references/sales-pitch.md` to build the 8-step pitch from it.

Positioning is the foundation; messaging and the sales pitch are built on top of it. Never write messaging or a pitch before the positioning underneath is settled — garbage in, garbage out.

## The core principle to hold throughout

Most weak positioning is not a wording problem. It is the result of **clinging to a default market frame rooted in where the product came from.** A team that set out to "build a better database" keeps calling it a database even after it has become something else — and in the database frame, its real strength looks like a minor feature while its weaknesses look damning. The first move is almost always to *let go of the origin story* and ask what the product actually is now. Carry this suspicion into every engagement: the market the user assumes they're in is frequently the wrong one.

## The 5 (+1) components of positioning

These are the building blocks. Work them roughly in this order, because each feeds the next. Do not jump to the market category first — it is the *output* of the earlier components, not the starting point.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would the customer use if your product did not exist? Crucially, this includes the status quo — a spreadsheet, a manual process, a junior hire, or **doing nothing at all.** "Do nothing" is usually the most common and most underrated alternative. Do not limit this to obvious head-to-head competitors; capture what the customer would *actually* fall back on.

2. **Unique (distinct) attributes.** What do you have that the alternatives lack? Features, yes, but also business model, architecture, integrations, supply chain, IP, a proprietary process. List everything that is genuinely yours, **without yet judging whether it's good or who cares.** Even an apparent weakness (complexity, opinionated-ness) can be a distinct attribute that becomes valuable in the right frame.

3. **Value (and value themes).** Translate each attribute into what it *does for the customer* — the "so what?". An attribute is "we query huge datasets in milliseconds"; the value is "answer a customer's question while they're still on the phone, instead of calling back tomorrow." Then **group related values into themes** (2–4 of them). The themes, not the raw feature list, are what positioning is built around.

4. **Best-fit customers (who cares a lot).** Many people get *some* value; only a subset care *intensely*. Identify the specific, findable characteristics of customers who care most — not "small businesses" (too broad) but the precise traits that make a prospect light up at your value. Best-fit customers buy faster, discount less, and churn less. Naming them tightly is how a small budget gets leverage.

5. **Market category (frame of reference).** Now choose the context that makes your differentiated value *obvious* to those best-fit customers. The right category points buyers toward what you do best **and away from what you don't do.** It is frequently NOT the category the product was born in. Test candidate frames by asking: in this frame, is our unique value the *main event* or a footnote? Does it invite a comparison we win or one we lose?

**(+1) Relevant trend.** Optionally, layer on a trend that makes the product feel timely and important *now* — but only if the link is direct and obvious. A forced trend ("AI-powered…" when it isn't the point) erodes trust. Use sparingly.

The throughline that connects all five is **differentiated value**: the value you and only you can deliver to a best-fit customer. It is the star. Everything else exists to make it obvious.

## Working process

Follow this sequence. With a user, do it as a guided interview — ask for what you don't have rather than inventing it; if the user wants you to draft from limited info, mark assumptions explicitly so they can correct them.

1. **Strip the baggage.** Ask what the product was originally built to be, then explicitly set that frame aside. State: "Let's not assume we're a {origin category} — we'll re-derive the category at the end."
2. **List true competitive alternatives** (including "do nothing" and manual workarounds).
3. **List unique attributes** vs. those alternatives — no judging yet.
4. **Map attributes → value → value themes.** Collapse into 2–4 themes.
5. **Define best-fit customers** — the specific traits of who cares most about those themes.
6. **Choose the market frame of reference** that centers the themes for those customers. Generate 2–3 candidate frames and pick deliberately (see Styles below).
7. **Optionally layer a trend** if it's a clean fit.
8. **Capture it** in the Positioning Canvas (below) — dated, structured, shareable.

## Output: the Positioning Canvas

Always capture the result in this structure. It is deliberately a structured set of named slots, not a prose paragraph or a fill-in-the-blank "positioning statement" (see why those fail, below). The structure is what makes positioning reviewable, testable, and usable as an input to downstream messaging.

```
# Positioning Canvas — {Product}
Date: {date}   |   Owner: {who}   |   Status: {draft / tested / approved}

Competitive alternatives:   {what they'd use otherwise, incl. "do nothing"}
Unique attributes:          {what we have that those lack}
Value themes:               {2–4 themes — the "so what" of the attributes}
Best-fit customers:         {specific, findable traits of who cares most}
Market category:            {the frame that makes our value obvious}
Relevant trend (optional):  {only if directly linked}

Differentiated value (one line): {the value we and only we deliver to best-fit customers}
Positioning style:          {head-to-head / big-fish-small-pond / new category}
```

Date it. Positioning decays as unique attributes become table stakes and markets shift, so the canvas is a living document to be reviewed, not a monument.

## Diagnosing weak or drifting positioning

Use these as symptoms when a user reports that their messaging "isn't landing" or their product "is hard to explain." Name the likely cause, then run the process to fix it.

- **"So what exactly is this for?"** asked repeatedly after a pitch → wrong or missing market frame; value isn't centered.
- **Stuck in a losing comparison** ("how are you different from {giant incumbent}?") → positioned in a category where a strong competitor owns the default value; reframe.
- **Long ramp before anyone "gets it"** → the frame isn't doing its job of setting fast context.
- **Sales and marketing tell different stories** → no shared, captured positioning; build the canvas as the single source of truth.
- **Best customers found you "by accident"** → your true best-fit segment differs from who you're targeting; revisit components 3–4.
- **Once-strong position now feels generic** → distinct attributes became table stakes; the market drifted under you. Re-derive.

A frequent root failure is **not understanding your own differentiated value**, which shows up three ways: *misrepresenting it* (claiming a value you can't uniquely own), *stretching it* (overclaiming compatibility/capability), or *not getting to the core of it* (stopping at a feature like "integrates with Salesforce" instead of the outcome it drives). Push to the core.

## The three styles of positioning

When choosing the market frame (step 6), pick a style deliberately — they imply very different strategies, budgets, and risks:

1. **Head-to-head** — compete to win an existing category outright, challenging the leader on its own terms. Only viable if you can genuinely claim to be the best in that category for a meaningful segment. High risk against entrenched leaders.
2. **Big fish, small pond** — carve out and dominate a sub-segment of an existing category that you can own, where your distinct attributes are decisive. Often the smart play for startups and focused products.
3. **Create a new category (or reframe the existing one)** — change how buyers think about the whole space, or define a genuinely new one. The most powerful when it works and the most expensive and difficult — it requires educating the market on a category that doesn't yet exist in their heads. Do not choose this lightly.

## Positioning is NOT messaging (the handoff)

This distinction matters and users routinely conflate them. **Positioning is the strategic decision** — the components and the chosen frame. **Messaging is the expression layer built on top of it**: approved value statements per buyer persona, taglines, and boilerplate that marketing and sales adapt. The canvas is the *input* to a messaging document, not the messaging itself.

Concretely: from an approved canvas, the value themes become persona-specific value statements; the market category sets the words used to introduce the product; the differentiated value becomes the headline claim; competitive alternatives shape the "why not X" talking points. When a user asks for messaging, first confirm the positioning is settled (or build it), then derive messaging from the named slots so every line traces back to a positioning decision rather than to taste. This traceability — messaging as a downstream artifact of a structured positioning spec — is the whole point of capturing positioning as named components rather than as prose.

## A compact worked example

A startup built a fast analytics engine, originally conceived as "a faster database."
- *Baggage:* calling it a "database" forced a comparison with entrenched incumbents, where its one strength (speed on huge datasets) read as a feature and its gaps read as disqualifying.
- *Alternatives:* the incumbent database they already owned; manual overnight batch reports; doing nothing.
- *Unique attributes:* sub-second queries over very large, machine-generated datasets.
- *Value theme:* answer urgent questions in real time instead of waiting for tomorrow's batch.
- *Best-fit customers:* teams analyzing massive machine-generated data under time pressure — e.g. fraud/security and ad-tech, not general data storage.
- *New frame:* reposition from "database" to "analytics / business intelligence," where real-time analysis is the *central* expected value rather than a side feature.
- *Result:* in the new frame the same product goes from "a weak database with one nice feature" to "an excellent analytics tool for a specific, urgent need." Nothing about the product changed — only the context did.
