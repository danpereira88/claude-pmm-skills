# Joanna Wiebe / Copyhackers Conversion Copywriting

> **Credit:** The method in this skill is Joanna Wiebe's and Copyhackers' (copyhackers.com). The awareness stages come from Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*. This skill is my own summary and working guide, written in my own words. See Copyhackers for the original material.

Joanna Wiebe coined the term "conversion copywriting." Her core claim, repeated throughout her work: **the best copy isn't written, it's found.** Great copy comes from swiping the actual words prospects and customers use — not from staring at a blank page and imagining what might persuade them. Apply that discipline here: research and evidence before invention, always.

## When to go deep vs. go light

- If the user gives you real research material (customer interviews, reviews, support/Gong transcripts, survey responses, testimonials) or you can gather it (search, uploaded files, past-chat context), run the full process below: mine it, then write from it.
- If there's no research available and the user just wants copy fast, say so briefly ("I don't have customer language to mine here, so I'll draft from the positioning/benefits you've given me — flag this to swap in real VoC quotes later") and proceed using the formulas with the best inputs available. Don't block the task waiting for research that isn't coming.
- Never fabricate quotes and present them as real customer language. Placeholder/illustrative language must be clearly marked as such.

## The 5-part conversion copywriting process

1. **Research & discovery** — Figure out the prospect's *stage of awareness* (below) and gather their actual words: interviews, surveys, reviews, support tickets, sales call transcripts, competitor reviews ("review mining"). Ask open questions, not yes/no ones. Wiebe's favorite: *"What was going on in your life that compelled you to come looking for a solution like this?"* — it surfaces the trigger, the motivation, and the emotional state in the prospect's own words.
2. **Synthesis** — Turn raw research into a messaging hierarchy: the one big idea, then supporting proof points, ranked by what matters most to the buyer (not what the company wants to say first).
3. **Wireframe / spit draft** — Block out the structure of the page or email with placeholder messages before polishing any sentence. Structure before style.
4. **Write** — Swipe sticky phrases straight from the research and combine them with proven formulas (below) to fill the wireframe. Most of the actual words should trace back to something a real prospect said.
5. **Test** — Split-test where possible. Copy is a hypothesis, not a verdict — treat launch as the start of iteration, not the end.

Read `references/message-mining.md` before running step 1 on any real research — it has the interview questions, the before-state/dream-state framework, and the review-mining technique in full.

## Stage of awareness — figure this out before writing anything

Borrowed from Eugene Schwartz and central to how Copyhackers targets copy. Match the opening of any page/email to where the prospect already is — don't start colder or warmer than the audience actually is:

1. **Unaware** — doesn't know they have the problem. Lead with the problem/story, not the product.
2. **Problem-aware** — knows the pain, doesn't know solutions exist. Agitate the pain, then introduce the category.
3. **Solution-aware** — knows solutions exist, hasn't found yours. Lead with mechanism/differentiation.
4. **Product-aware** — knows your product, hasn't decided. Lead with proof, objections, differentiators.
5. **Most aware** — ready to buy, just needs the offer. Lead with the deal/CTA — don't bury it in a pitch they already believe.

Ask the user (or infer from context — traffic source, funnel stage, existing copy) which stage applies before drafting. Getting this wrong is the single most common reason copy misses.

## Buyer modalities (for homepage/broad-audience copy)

When you don't know who's landing on the page (homepages especially), Wiebe borrows Eisenberg's four buyer types and writes to all of them across the page, not just one:

- **Competitive** — wants to know you're better than alternatives; responds to comparisons, "vs." framing, competitive proof.
- **Spontaneous** — decides fast on emotion; responds to urgency, big bold promise, minimal friction.
- **Humanistic** — decides on relationship/story; responds to people, faces, testimonials, "who this is for."
- **Methodical** — decides on logic/detail; responds to specs, data, FAQs, how-it-works.

On a landing page (single traffic source, known intent), you don't need all four — write to the modality that fits that audience.

## Before-state / Dream-state (use for any transformation-based copy)

Frame the value prop as a transformation, anchored in the prospect's own words wherever you have them:

- **Before state**: the pain, friction, anxiety, or status quo the prospect is in now.
- **Dream state**: the outcome/identity they want, in their language, not just the feature list.

Good copy makes the gap between the two vivid, then positions the product as the bridge.

## Editing pass — "write like a human"

Before finalizing any draft, check it against these Wiebe-isms:

- **Read it aloud.** If you wouldn't say it to a friend, cut it.
- **Kill the throat-clearing.** Cut warm-up sentences before the point. Lead with the point.
- **One idea per sentence/section.** Don't stack benefits into a run-on.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** "Saves you 4 hours a week" beats "increases efficiency."
- **The CTA should restate the value, not just say "Submit."** E.g. "Start my free trial" not "Submit."
- **Cut adjectives that aren't doing proof work.** "Powerful," "innovative," "seamless" mean nothing without evidence attached.
- **Every claim earns its place with proof** — a stat, a quote, a mechanism, or a name-drop — or it gets cut or softened.

## Output format

When delivering copy:
1. State which awareness stage and (if relevant) buyer modality you wrote to, and why.
2. Give the copy itself, clearly sectioned (headline / subhead / body / CTA, or section-by-section for a full page).
3. Flag anywhere you used placeholder language instead of real VoC quotes, so the user knows what to swap in later.
4. Optionally: offer 2-3 headline/CTA variants for testing rather than a single locked answer — testing is core to the methodology.
