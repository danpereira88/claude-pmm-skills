# Message Mining & Voice-of-Customer Research

Core idea: don't invent copy, find it. Real prospects and customers have already told you — in interviews, reviews, support tickets, sales calls — exactly what language moves them. Your job is to extract it, not paraphrase it into something blander.

## Sources to mine (roughly best to weakest signal)

1. **Sales call transcripts / Gong recordings** — prospects describing their problem unprompted, in their own words, under no pressure to sound polished. Gold for objections and trigger moments.
2. **Customer interviews** — one-on-one, open-ended, with existing customers, churned customers, and prospects who didn't convert.
3. **Support tickets / win-loss notes** — pain language and objection language, respectively.
4. **Surveys** — good for scale, weaker for nuance (people give shorter, more guarded answers in writing).
5. **Review mining** — reviews of your product, competitor products, or even adjacent/analogous products (e.g. Wiebe's rehab-center headline was swiped from a book review about addiction, not from any rehab-specific source). Use when you don't have direct customer access.

## The one killer interview/survey question

> "What was going on in your life that compelled you to come looking for a solution like this?"

This single open question reliably surfaces: the trigger event, the emotional state, the stakes, and the internal narrative the prospect was telling themselves — all in their own words. Prefer it over demographic or feature-preference questions, which don't give you sticky language.

Other useful open questions:
- "What almost stopped you from buying / signing up?" (objections, in their words)
- "What would you tell a friend who was on the fence about this?" (best-case pitch, in their words)
- "What's the #1 thing this has changed for you?" (dream-state outcome, in their words)
- "What did you try before this, and why didn't it work?" (competitive alternative + gap)

Open-ended > yes/no. You're not just trying to learn facts about the prospect — you're trying to capture the phrases they reach for.

## Workflow

1. **Collect.** Get all raw quotes into one place (a spreadsheet works better than a doc — you'll want to filter/sort). One quote per row, with source noted.
2. **Tag by state.** Simplest split: **Before state** (pain, friction, anxiety, status quo complaints) vs. **Dream state** (desired outcome, identity, relief, in their words). A finer-grained tag set: pains, anxieties/objections, desired outcomes, trigger events, language/vocabulary quirks, feature requests.
3. **Cluster into topics.** Group similar quotes together — patterns will emerge (e.g. multiple people independently describing the same fear or the same "aha" moment). The topics that recur most are your strongest candidates for headline/section themes.
4. **Extract sticky phrases.** Within each cluster, pull the most vivid, specific, quotable phrasing — not the most representative-sounding one. Specificity and unusual phrasing beat generic paraphrase almost every time.
5. **Map to page structure.** Before-state clusters tend to map to the problem/agitation sections; dream-state clusters map to the promise/outcome sections and testimonials; objection clusters map to the objection-handling section or FAQ.

## What "swiping" means in practice

Swiping doesn't mean copying customer words verbatim without attribution or context — it means using a real prospect's actual phrase (sometimes near-verbatim, sometimes lightly tightened) as your headline, subhead, or proof point instead of writing something more generic that a copywriter would come up with alone. The bar: if a real customer would read the line and think "yes, exactly, that's what I meant," it's working. If it reads like brand-speak, it isn't.

When you have sales-call recordings, win/loss notes or pipeline notes, mine them the same way: how prospects describe why deals were won or lost, the buyer framing they use, and the objections raised in competitive deals. Extract sticky before/dream-state phrases rather than summarizing them.
