# Swipeable Formulas (Headlines, Hero Sections, Pages, Emails, CTAs)

Use these as structural starting points, then fill them with real voice-of-customer language (see message-mining.md) rather than invented copy.

## Headline formulas

- **Fill-in-the-blank outcome**: "[Achieve dream outcome] without [common pain/tradeoff]" — e.g. "Enterprise-grade reporting without a data team."
- **Direct address of the trigger**: name the exact moment/situation that brought them here (from message mining) — e.g. "If you think you need rehab, you do." (Wiebe's own >400%-lift example — swiped from a book review, not written from scratch.)
- **Mechanism-led** (for solution-aware/product-aware audiences): "[Product] uses [mechanism] to [outcome]" — leads with *how*, which builds credibility once the reader already believes the problem is real.
- **Specific-number claim**: concrete, testable numbers beat vague superlatives — "cuts month-end close from 10 days to 3" beats "faster close."

## Hero section formula (above the fold)

The hero section has to do a lot in a little space. A reliable skeleton:

1. **Headline** — the one big promise or the trigger/pain, matched to awareness stage.
2. **Subhead** — the mechanism, differentiator, or "how," resolving "ok but how?" in one line.
3. **Supporting visual** — product screenshot, diagram, or (for humanistic modality) a face/customer.
4. **CTA** — value-restating button text, not generic "Submit"/"Learn more."
5. **Micro-proof** — a logo bar, stat, or one-line credibility signal near the CTA (reduces the "can I trust this" hesitation right at the decision point).

Write the hero to the dominant buyer modality for that traffic source (competitive / spontaneous / humanistic / methodical — see SKILL.md). On a homepage with mixed traffic, it's common to layer more than one hero-adjacent section so different modalities each get a moment.

## PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solve) — good default for problem-aware audiences

1. **Problem** — name it plainly, ideally in the prospect's own words.
2. **Agitate** — make the cost of the status quo vivid and specific (time lost, risk carried, deals lost) — don't rush past this; agitation is what creates urgency for a solution.
3. **Solve** — introduce the product as the resolution, tied directly back to the specific agitation just raised (not a generic feature list).

## Long-form sales page skeleton (Schwartz/Copyhackers "10x Sales Pages" structure)

1. Headline + subhead — biggest promise, matched to awareness stage.
2. Problem/agitation — the before-state, vivid and specific.
3. Empathy/credibility — "we get it because..." — establishes the writer/brand understands the reader's exact situation.
4. Introduce the solution — name it, state the core promise, tie back to the moment of highest tension just established.
5. Reveal the full offer — everything included, the mechanism, how it works.
6. Features → benefits — every feature explicitly tied to an outcome, never listed bare.
7. Proof stack — testimonials, case studies, stats, logos — distributed throughout, not just parked at the bottom.
8. Objection handling — address the real objections surfaced in research (see below), not strawman ones.
9. Offer recap + urgency/scarcity if genuine.
10. CTA, restated value, final proof nudge.

Long sales pages earn their length only when every section is doing real persuasive work traced back to research — padding with generic claims defeats the purpose.

## Objection handling

Pull real objections from message mining (sales call transcripts and win/loss notes are the best source — e.g. deals lost to a specific competitor, or specific pushback on price/security/integration). Structure each objection as:

- Name the objection plainly (don't dodge it or bury it).
- Acknowledge why it's a reasonable concern.
- Resolve it with a specific proof point (data, mechanism, guarantee, customer quote) — not just reassurance.

Objections a reader is silently holding and never sees addressed are one of the most common reasons copy that reads well still doesn't convert.

## Email formulas

- **Subject line**: curiosity or specific-benefit, matched to awareness stage; avoid generic "Newsletter #12" framing.
- **Opening line**: continue the promise from the subject line immediately — don't make the reader wait for the point.
- **Body**: one idea, one CTA. Multi-CTA emails dilute response.
- **P.S.**: a strong, often-overlooked slot — restate the single strongest proof point or the deadline/urgency; P.S. lines get disproportionate read-through.

## CTA / microcopy rules

- Button copy should restate the value the reader gets, in the first person or imperative — "Start my free trial," not "Submit" or generic "Learn more."
- Reduce perceived friction right next to the CTA: "No credit card required," "2-minute setup," a security/compliance badge — whatever objection is strongest at that exact decision point.
- Match CTA intensity to awareness stage: "most aware" traffic can take a hard "Buy now"; colder traffic often converts better on a lower-commitment CTA ("See how it works" before "Book a demo").
